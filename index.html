Research update · 10 November 2026

Learn anatomy once. Plan for every patient.

MedSpatial is building a shared 3D understanding of the human skeleton. It learns from scans nobody had to label, so surgical planning and patient-specific devices can be built from far fewer expert cases.

The problem

The scans exist. The labels don’t.

Patient-specific surgery runs on 3D anatomy: where the fragments of a fracture belong, where a screw can safely go, what shape an implant or an appliance must take.

Teaching software any of that normally takes hundreds of cases labelled by experts, and most specialties will never have them. Hospitals, meanwhile, hold enormous numbers of scans that no one has labelled for any task.

MedSpatial began in cleft care, designing infant molding appliances from a few dozen intraoral scans. A few dozen scans cannot teach a model what 3D anatomy is from scratch. They should only have to teach what is specific to cleft care.

The idea

One model of anatomy, shared by every task.

A surgeon does not relearn the pelvis for each new procedure. MedSpatial gives software the same head start: learn the shapes and relationships of anatomy once, from many unlabelled scans, then adapt to each clinical task with a small number of expert cases.

How it learns

Practise on bones nobody labelled. Then learn the task.

The model first teaches itself what bone surfaces look like by filling in pieces we hide from it. Only then does it see a handful of expert-labelled cases for the task at hand.

  1. Step 1 · Scans

    Ordinary CT scans

    Existing tools already outline each bone. The main pool is 1,228 public whole-body CTs (TotalSegmentator).

  2. Step 2 · Surfaces

    Bones become points

    Each bone's surface becomes a cloud of points measured in millimetres. This person has 62 bones and about 185,000 surface points.

  3. Step 3 · Practice, no labels

    Fill in what is hidden

    The surface is cut into small patches and 60% are hidden (teal). The model learns by rebuilding them. Nobody has to label anything.

  4. Step 4 · The task

    A few labelled cases

    The practised model is then fine-tuned on 10 or 50 expert-labelled cases: here, naming each rib; elsewhere, putting fracture fragments back in place.

The hardest test so far · Route 14

Practise on everything except the ribs and pelvis. Then ask about ribs and pelvis.

To check that the model learns anatomy in general rather than memorising one bone, a model called X practised on 914 people with every rib, costal cartilage, hip bone and the sacrum left out. X still helped on both tasks it had never seen: labelling ribs and reducing pelvic fractures.

Progress

From nothing working to real patients in four weeks.

The first application is pelvic fracture reduction: putting each broken piece back where it belongs. It is hard, it is measurable, and a public set of real cases with surgeon-verified answers exists to score against.

On 26 October the 170 real cases were split: 82 for working and tuning, 88 sealed and scored once per finished model. This chart stops there. Results on the sealed half, down to 6.6 mm, are under Evidence.

    Started on synthetic shapes

    About seventy small experiments on spheres, cones and tori. Nothing carried over, and an outside review showed the test could not tell good models from bad. We moved to real anatomy.

    The founding idea, shown on real patients

    Pretraining on unlabelled pelvis scans made fracture reduction more accurate, under a rule written before the run, in both independent runs. With longer training: 7.3 mm, 51% of fragments placed.

  10. 26 Oct

    The real cases split in two

    82 fractures for working and tuning, 88 sealed and scored once per finished model. The same day, a combined training objective did not improve fractures (Route 6).

  11. 27 Oct–2 Nov

    Bigger pelvis models stopped helping

    Across four model sizes the gain per step shrank from 0.37 to 0.01 mm on the practice task, and the largest did not reduce fractures better (Routes 7, 7b).

    Ruled out
  12. 28 Oct

    A second anatomy: labelling ribs

    Practice on 782 unlabelled rib cages lifted rib labelling on a sealed test of 160 cases: 0.89 overlap from 10 labelled cases, against 0.74 from scratch (Route 8).

    Result
  13. 30 Oct–2 Nov

    A more realistic fracture simulator

    Training on more lifelike simulated fractures made the real ones worse: 8.3 and 9.4 mm against 7.7 mm on the working half (Routes 11, 11b).

    Ruled out
  14. 31 Oct

    One model for every bone, first attempt

    A whole-skeleton model that also learned surface maps fell well short of the rib specialist: 0.78 overlap against 0.89 (Route 9).

    Ruled out
  15. 2 Nov

    Bone identity, learned with no labels at all

    Keeping only the fill-in-the-hidden-patches practice, the whole-skeleton model tells bone types apart 88% of the time, up from 67%, level with the rib specialist (Route 13).

    Result
  16. 3 Nov

    A refinement pass, confirmed on the sealed half

    Teaching the model to refine its own answer beat the earlier best on the 88 sealed cases in both runs: 7.3 and 7.1 mm against 7.8 and 7.9 (Route 10b). Refining for longer missed its pass mark (Route 15).

    Result
  17. 5 Nov

    It helps anatomy it never saw

    A model that practised without any rib or pelvis still beat training from scratch on both: rib labelling and fracture reduction (Route 14).

    Result
  18. 5 Nov

    Fine-tuning on real cases: best model so far

    The refinement model, fine-tuned on the 82 working cases, reached 6.7 and 6.5 mm on the 88 sealed ones, 0.59 mm better in both runs (Route 17).

    Result
  19. 6 Nov

    Not just more training

    Given three times the training, a model from scratch still trailed the pretrained model trained just as long by 0.7 mm, and only tied one trained a third as long (Route 18).

    Result
  20. 10 Nov

    More people, or a bigger model?

    Practising on more people helped fractures but not ribs. A model about eight times larger helped neither, and no general model reached the rib specialist (Route 19).

    Mixed
  21. 10 Nov

    Bones only, or bones plus soft tissue?

    Four new encoders trained, their rib runs scored once on the sealed test, fracture runs under way in the cloud. No verdict yet (Route 21).

    Running
  22. 10 Nov

    A harder practice task, and gentler fine-tuning

    Rebuilding whole hidden bones as well as patches (Route 20), and lower fine-tuning rates for the large model (Route 22). Both running on the laptop.

    Running
  23. Next

    A pelvis top-up for the general model

    Approved, to start after Route 22: a short extra round of practice on pelvis scans, to see whether the general model can catch the pelvis specialist (Route 23).

How we work

Built to be believed.

Every experiment states its question, its controls and its pass mark before it runs. The result is recorded whether it is good news or not.

119experiment protocols and pre-registrations written before the runs they describe
84results reports, negative results included. Each pre-registration also records its own result.
5sealed test sets, never used for tuning. Each finished model is scored once on the set for its task. Two have never been opened.
1laptop runs every model. Training used that laptop and free cloud notebooks.

  • Controls that catch wishful thinking

    Every pretrained model is compared with the identical model started from scratch, so a gain from the architecture is never mistaken for a gain from learning.

  • Failures are kept

    More than a dozen routes that did not work are documented as carefully as the ones that did. Each one narrowed down what the next experiment should try.

  • Local first

    Models run on a single machine with no cloud AI service in the loop, which suits hospitals that keep patient data on site.

What it could become

One foundation. Many procedures.

Many procedures share one structure: current anatomy, plus a goal, plus a device, gives a design. If one model of anatomy helps several of them, it stops being a project and becomes infrastructure.

The proving ground for the approach, on public research data.

From a clinical goal to a validated design.

The long-term system pairs the anatomy model with design tools and checks. An agent proposes a step, a geometry engine builds it, and a deterministic validator accepts or rejects it. Nothing counts as done until a validator passes it, and every operation is versioned like code.

InputClinical goal

“Restore the pelvic ring” or “an appliance that guides the cleft closed over six weeks”.

InputPatient spatial model

Bones, vessels, soft tissue and landmarks of this patient, from MedSpatial.

OutputValidated, versioned design

A plan, implant, guide or appliance, with the full history of how it was made and which checks it passed.

Where we stand

Early, measured, and specific about the gaps.

  • Research stage. Nothing here is a clinical claim, and MedSpatial is not a medical device.
  • Not yet the most accurate. The best published fracture systems still have lower error. They are scored on a different, hidden test set and train on the real cases we keep for testing, so the numbers do not compare directly.
  • Two anatomies so far. Pretraining has helped pelvic fractures and rib labelling, even when the model never saw either. A general model still trails the specialists pretrained on ribs or pelves.
  • Bigger is not better yet. A model about eight times larger improved neither task. Practising on more people helped fractures but not ribs.
  • Small fragments are the frontier. The model improves every size of fragment, but the smallest pieces still carry the most error.
